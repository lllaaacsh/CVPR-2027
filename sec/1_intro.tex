\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large video generation models~\cite{nvidia2025cosmos}, trained on vast amounts of video, capture how the visual world looks and how it changes over time. World action models (WAMs)~\cite{pai2025mimicvideo,ye2026dreamzero} bring this knowledge to robot control by modeling the future of the scene together with the robot's actions:
a pretrained video model predicts how the scene will evolve under an instruction, and the robot's actions are derived from this predicted future,
typically by an action decoder that reads the internal representation of the video model.
Such policies inherit visual and physical priors from video pretraining, rather than learning them from robot demonstrations alone.

A video model, however, is built to generate video.
Its representation of the future covers every region of every frame on a dense, uniform grid, because generation must render the entire scene.
Acting in the scene asks for something narrower: the next actions depend mostly on the robot, the objects it interacts with~\cite{bendikas2025oatvla}, and the space it is about to move through. Yet the action decoder receives the full, generation-shaped representation, and the video model computes all of it at full resolution at every policy query, regardless of which parts the action actually uses; this computation accounts for most of the cost of acting.

Recent work has asked whether the future must be generated at test time at all~\cite{yuan2026fastwam} or rolled out step by step~\cite{zhang2026rift}, but whether a policy needs the predicted future at the resolution at which it would be generated, and which parts of it the action actually relies on, has received far less attention.

A natural tool for making such a representation more compact is token reduction, which merges tokens that look alike~\cite{bolya2023tome} or discards tokens that receive little attention~\cite{chen2024fastv}.
These criteria, however, describe the image rather than the task: they measure whether a token is redundant in appearance or salient to the network, not whether the action depends on it.
Cues specific to robots, such as recent end-effector motion~\cite{pei2026adp}, help decide when to reduce tokens but not which tokens the action depends on.

First, it arrives too late to guide the pass that produces it: the decoder attends to features that the video model produces late in its forward pass, so attention exists only after every token of the future has been computed at full resolution.
Methods that select tokens by attention therefore take it from an earlier denoising step~\cite{kim2026cfprune,xie2026sparsewam} or earlier control steps~\cite{liu2025vlapruner}, and still pay for at least one full-resolution pass to obtain it.
Second, attention shows where the decoder looks, which need not coincide with how much its prediction would change if a region were represented more coarsely~\cite{serrano2019attention}, and it is the latter that decides whether a region can be coarsened.

Building on this idea \ziquancomment{which idea?}, we propose \method, an action-aware router for WAMs (\cref{fig:overview}).
A lightweight router, placed after an early block of the frozen video model, scores every token of the predicted future by its relevance to the action.
It is supervised only through the action decoder:
its training signal is the gradient of the decoder's objective with respect to the scores, and no term depends on appearance or similarity (\cref{sec:router}).
These scores guide spatial merging and temporal aggregation under a fixed token budget.
Only the router and a low-rank adapter in the action decoder are trained, and the adapter can be merged into the decoder after training.
Because the video model and the decoder then process fewer tokens, the same allocation lowers the cost of every policy query.

On the three LIBERO task suites~\cite{liu2023libero}, \method keeps between 10\% and 30\% of the future tokens. At 20\% (30\% of all tokens) it remains statistically indistinguishable from the policy that reads the full representation, while a policy query takes 440.7\,ms instead of 701.7\,ms ($1.59\times$).
Further analysis shows that the regions it keeps at full resolution are necessary for the policy and concentrate on the robot and the objects it manipulates.

In summary, our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item An action-aware router for WAMs, supervised only through the objective of the action decoder, which identifies the parts of the predicted future that the action relies on.
  \item A spatial resolution allocation that keeps the most relevant regions at full resolution and merges the others instead of removing them, keeping every token at its original position. \ziquancomment{is this counted as a contribution? and it is unclear what this contribution is compared with existing work}
  \item A temporal resolution allocation that gives each region a number of time segments set by its relevance, with consecutive temporal positions.
\end{itemize}
