\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
    \ji{
    World action models (WAMs)~\cite{pai2025mimicvideo,hu2025vpp,ma2026dit4dit,ye2026dreamzero} leverage pre-trained video foundation models~\cite{nvidia2025cosmos,wan2025wan} to transfer visual and physical priors to robotic manipulation.
    At each control step, a video diffusion transformer (DiT) processes observed frames and noisy future tokens through a stack of transformer blocks, and an action decoder cross-attends to the resulting future features to predict robot actions~\cite{pai2025mimicvideo,ma2026dit4dit}.
    Because video DiTs are designed for full-scene pixel synthesis, they represent future frames on a dense, uniform spatiotemporal token grid.
    Even in single-pass WAMs that avoid iterative denoising~\cite{pai2025mimicvideo,zhang2026rift}, processing this dense future grid across all DiT blocks incurs substantial inference latency.
    However, robotic control relies unevenly on the predicted future: accurate manipulation requires fine detail around the robot arm, target objects~\cite{bendikas2025oatvla}, and motion trajectories, whereas static surroundings only provide coarse spatial layout.
    }

    \ji{
    With a frozen video DiT, token reduction must occur after a shallow block so that all remaining blocks process fewer future tokens.
    However, existing token reduction methods face three key limitations in single-pass WAMs.
    First, visual token merging and pruning methods~\cite{bolya2023tome,li2024vidtome,chen2024fastv,hyun2025sttm} score tokens using feature similarity or shallow self-attention, which reflect appearance redundancy rather than action relevance.
    Second, action-guided pruning methods~\cite{liu2025vlapruner,pei2026adp,xie2026sparsewam,kim2026cfprune} score tokens via action-decoder cross-attention or robot motion gates, yet the action decoder operates only after all video DiT blocks complete.
    Such methods therefore require an extra full-resolution pass or dense fallback queries~\cite{liu2025vlapruner,pei2026adp,kim2026cfprune} that offset single-pass speedups, and attention weights do not directly measure action sensitivity to coarsening~\cite{serrano2019attention}.
    Meanwhile, end-to-end differentiable selectors~\cite{brohan2023rt1,jiang2025lightvla} avoid extra passes during inference, but require updating the visual backbone.
    Third, pruning-based approaches~\cite{chen2024fastv,yang2025efficientvla,liu2025vlapruner,kim2026cfprune} delete unselected tokens entirely, erasing coarse workspace layout and creating gapped temporal indices incompatible with pre-trained positional priors.
    }

    \ji{
    To overcome these limitations, we propose \textbf{A}ction-aware \textbf{R}esolution \textbf{RO}uting for \textbf{W}orld action models (\method), a multi-resolution framework for single-pass WAMs with frozen video backbones (\cref{fig:teaser}c).
    \method inserts a lightweight, instruction-conditioned router after a shallow block of the frozen video DiT to predict per-token action relevance in a single forward pass.
    During training, the router is supervised solely by the gradient of the action decoder's objective, directly identifying regions where coarsening would perturb the predicted action.
    Under a fixed token budget, these relevance scores guide two-stage spatiotemporal resolution allocation without discarding any region of the scene.
    In the spatial stage, \method retains high-scoring patch groups at full resolution and locally averages remaining groups on native grid coordinates, preserving spatial alignment with observed frames.
    In the temporal stage, \method pools each spatial tubelet into contiguous segments weighted by action relevance and feature dynamics, and assigns consecutive temporal indices for the frozen DiT.
    Finally, we adapt the flow-matching action decoder~\cite{lipman2023flow} to compressed representations via velocity distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling} using a low-rank adapter (LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora}, which merges into the decoder weights after training with zero inference overhead. 
    }

    \ji{
    We evaluate \method on two WAM backbones (\texttt{mimic-video}~\cite{pai2025mimicvideo} and \texttt{DiT4DiT}~\cite{ma2026dit4dit}) across LIBERO~\cite{liu2023libero}, SIMPLER-Bridge, and RoboCasa-GR1.
    On \texttt{mimic-video}, \method at a 20\% future-token budget achieves 90.5\% mean success on LIBERO (vs.\ 91.5\% full-context) with a 1.59$\times$ speedup (440.7\,ms vs.\ 701.7\,ms), and reaches 92.9\% success (1.47$\times$ speedup) at a 30\% budget.
    Segmentation and distortion analyses confirm that full-resolution groups cover 90\% of task objects, 84\% of the robot arm, and 82\% of the future arm path, outperforming task-agnostic selection criteria.
    In real-world evaluations across six Franka kitchen tasks, \method improves mean success from 60.0\% to 77.8\% while accelerating policy inference by 1.84$\times$.
    }

    Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item We propose \method, an action-aware resolution routing framework for frozen single-pass WAMs, supervised directly by action-decoder gradients and paired with velocity-distilled decoder adaptation.
        \item We introduce a context-preserving spatiotemporal allocator that locally merges low-relevance spatial groups on native coordinates and aggregates dynamics-aware temporal tubelets with consecutive indices.
        \item We demonstrate consistent speedups and strong control performance across two WAM backbones, three simulation benchmarks, and six real-world robotic manipulation tasks.
    \end{itemize}

Recent work has asked whether the future must be generated at test time at all~\cite{yuan2026fastwam} or rolled out step by step~\cite{zhang2026rift}, but whether a policy needs the predicted future at the resolution at which it would be generated, and which parts of it the action actually relies on, has received far less attention.

A region may receive high attention even when representing it more coarsely barely changes the action. Consequently, existing methods either use image-centric criteria that ignore action relevance or rely on attention signals that are both delayed and indirect. We instead learn to allocate computation based on its impact on action prediction, using the decoder’s objective to supervise an early router that preserves fine-grained tokens where needed and coarse representations elsewhere.
