\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}


\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pipelinev2}
   \caption{\textbf{Overview of \method.}
(a) Overal pipeline. An early action-aware router allocates spatiotemporal resolution within the frozen video backbone before decoding actions. (b) In ARROW, the router predicts token action relevance, which guides spatial resolution allocation and dynamics-aware temporal allocation.}
   \label{fig:overview}
\end{figure*}


The overview of \method is shown in~\cref{fig:overview}. \method processes future regions at different spatial and temporal resolutions within a single forward pass of the frozen video DiT. An instruction-conditioned router uses features from an early block to predict per-token action relevance and guide resolution allocation under a fixed token budget. We train the router solely through the action decoder's objective and adapt the decoder through distillation from the full-context policy.



\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

In WAM, video backbones process dense future representations at uniform spatiotemporal resolution, although their relevance to action prediction can vary across regions and time intervals.
At every policy query, the video model receives an instruction, observed frames, and noisy future latents.
Let $Z$ denote the future tokens after a shallow block of the video backbone, and $f(Z)$ the action predicted by the remaining backbone and decoder.
We seek an allocation $A$ that represents $Z$ with a fixed budget of $N$ future tokens while minimizing deviation from the full-context action prediction:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{A,\,\phi}\ \mathbb{E}\,\big\| f_\phi\big(A(Z)\big) - f(Z) \big\|^2
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad |A(Z)| = N ,
  \label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}
where $|A(Z)|$ is the number of tokens after allocation, $N\leq THW$ is the retained future-token budget, $f_\phi$ augments $f$ with a low-rank decoder adapter, and ``s.t.'' stands for ``subject to''. $A$ only averages neighboring tokens spatially or over consecutive frames, and hence every part of the scene remains represented at different resolutions.

\subsection{Action-Aware Router}
\label{sec:router}
Resolution allocation is performed after a shallow block of the frozen video backbone and thus requires action-relevance estimates before the full video forward pass is processed.
We introduce a lightweight router supervised by the action decoder to provide these estimates.
Given early future tokens, each token attends to the instruction through one cross-attention layer, followed by an MLP and sigmoid that output $s_{t,p}\in[0,1]$.
Here $t$ and $p$ are indices of future time steps and spatial positions, respectively.
% (\cref{sec:spatial,sec:temporal}).

The router is trained on the demonstrations of the target task suite, i.e. the same data used to train the policy.
Video features are computed as at inference, with the future frames at the inference noise level and the compression applied,
so the router learns on the inputs it will see when deployed.
The router is jointly trained with a LoRA adapter~\cite{hu2022lora} on the cross-attention layers of the action decoder, which allows the decoder adjust to the compressed input.

We use a flow matching policy~\cite{lipman2023flow} as the action decoder, which predicts a velocity for a noised action chunk.
The adapter is trained on the compressed context to match the output of the same decoder evaluated on the full context, with a lightly weighted ground-truth loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} =
  \underbrace{\norm{v - v_{\text{full}}}^2}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}}}
  + \, \lambda_{\text{GT}} \underbrace{\norm{v - v_{\text{gt}}}^2}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{GT}}} ,
  \label{eq:task_loss}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is the velocity predicted by the adapted decoder from the compressed context,
$v_{\text{full}}$ is the velocity predicted by the original decoder from the full context for the same noised action and robot state,
and $v_{\text{gt}}$ is the ground-truth velocity. We set $\lambda_{\text{GT}}=0.02$ to keep the ground-truth supervision weak relative to distillation.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}}$ makes the adapted decoder reproduce the original decoder's full-context prediction, while $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GT}}$ anchors it to the demonstrated ground-truth velocity.

The router learns which groups of tokens (\cref{sec:spatial}) should keep full resolution under a fixed token budget.
Its supervision is the gradient saliency of the decoder objective with respect to the group scores:
\begin{equation}
  r_g = \max\Big(0,\, -\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}}{\partial S_g}\Big),
  \label{eq:saliency}
\end{equation}
where $S_g$ is the score of group $g$ (\cref{eq:group_score}) and $r_g$ measures how much raising it would lower $\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}$.
Keeping a group at full resolution is a discrete choice, so we multiply its tokens by a straight-through factor~\cite{bengio2013estimating} that equals one in the forward pass; this makes the gradient defined without changing the forward computation.
We smooth $r_g$ with an exponential moving average over training steps, label the top-$k$ groups as relevant and the rest as irrelevant, where $k$ is the number of groups kept at full resolution, and train the router on these labels with
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{router}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} + \lambda_b \mathcal{L}_{\text{budget}} + \lambda_e \mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}} ,
  \label{eq:router_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}$ is a focal binary cross-entropy~\cite{lin2017focal} between the scores and the labels,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{budget}}$ keeps the mean score at the fraction of groups kept at full resolution,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}$ pushes the scores towards 0 or 1, and $\lambda_b = \lambda_e = 0.1$. We jointly train the router and decoder LoRA by minimizing
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{router}}$.
The task loss trains the decoder LoRA and guides the router
through the straight-through surrogate, while the router loss
provides additional pseudo-label supervision and regularization.

\subsection{Spatial Resolution Allocation}
\label{sec:spatial}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{temporal_spatial_detail.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Spatial and temporal allocation.}
(a) Full-resolution and merged $2{\times}2$ spatial groups.
(b) Temporal segmentations at the 20\% future-token budget.
Segment boundaries are schematic. }
\label{fig:allocation_detail}
\end{figure}

A region may tolerate spatial coarsening while still providing useful context for control, such as the approximate location of a nearby object.
Discarding the region's tokens also removes its spatial context.
We therefore retain high-relevance regions at full resolution and locally average the remaining regions, preserving a coarse representation. Specifically, we partition the spatial grid into $G=HW/4$ non-overlapping $2\times2$ groups, with each group receiving one score from the router:
\begin{equation}
  S_g = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{p \in g} \max_t \, s_{t,p} ,
  \label{eq:group_score}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is a group, $p$ runs over its four positions, and $s_{t,p}$ is the router score of \cref{sec:router}.
Taking the maximum over the future frames means that a position counts as relevant if it matters in any frame;
averaging over the group gives its overall relevance.
During training, the router supervision of \cref{sec:router} is computed on these group scores.

We rank the groups by $S_g$.
The $F$ highest-scoring groups retain their four tubes, while each remaining group is averaged frame by frame into one tube.
This produces $P=G+3F$ tubes.

Every tube keeps its position on the original grid; a merged tube takes the position $p$ of the top-left tube of its group,
so every remaining tube is still identified by a grid position $p$.
We do not re-index the tubes onto a smaller grid:
the observation frames stay on the original grid, so a future tube at position $p$ still refers to the same part of the scene as the observed tokens at $p$.

\subsection{Temporal Resolution Allocation}
\label{sec:temporal}

Spatial resolution allocation reduces redundancy within each frame, while substantial redundancy remains across future frames. To further reduce temporal redundancy, we divide each tube into \(K_p\) contiguous segments based on action relevance and feature variation, and average the tokens within each segment.

Each tube $p$ receives a priority
\begin{equation}
  u_p = S_{g(p)} \cdot \Delta_p ,
  \label{eq:priority}
\end{equation}
where $S_{g(p)}$ is the score of the group $g(p)$ that contains position $p$,
and $\Delta_p$ is the average change of the features of tube $p$ between consecutive frames, measured as one minus their cosine similarity.
A tube thus ranks high when it is relevant to the action and its features change across frames.
We give the most segments to the tubes with the highest priority, with a fixed number of tubes at each level (\cref{fig:allocation_detail}b).
The segment counts satisfy $\sum_{p=1}^{P}K_p=N$, with $1\leq K_p\leq T$.
Within a tube, segment boundaries are placed so that each segment covers a similar amount of feature change.

Every tube retains at least one token, and pooling incorporates contributions from all $T$ future positions.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{contiguous_t.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Temporal positions after segment pooling.}
Source-frame indices (source-t) and consecutive segment indices
(contiguous-t) for the same pooled tube at the 20\% future-token budget.}
\label{fig:temporal_positions}
\end{figure}

The $k$-th segment of every tube takes the $k$-th future time position, whichever frames it covers, so the temporal positions stay consecutive as in the original sequence.
Unlike spatial coordinates, which preserve alignment with the observations (\cref{sec:spatial}), consecutive temporal indices preserve segment order and match the position pattern used during pretraining; source-frame indices would leave gaps (\cref{fig:temporal_positions}).
