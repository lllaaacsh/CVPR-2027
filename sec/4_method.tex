\cref{sec:analysis} showed that the token grid of a world action model is shaped by video generation, while the action decoder depends on only part of it, and that neither similarity-based selection nor deletion recovers that part at the deployment point.
\method reshapes the grid according to what the action needs (\cref{fig:overview}).
It decides, per region of the scene, at which spatial and temporal resolution the backbone computes, using relevance learned from the action decoder itself.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The video DiT processes $T = T_o + T_f$ latent frames on an $H{\times}W$ grid of $P = HW$ tokens per frame:
$T_o$ observation frames and $T_f$ future frames, which at the deployment point are noise placeholders (\cref{sec:analysis}).
Let $X_o$ denote the observation tokens and $X_f \in \R^{T_f \times P \times D}$ the future tokens after block $\ell_c$.
\method replaces $X_f$ with a compact set of $N_f$ tokens, leaves $X_o$ untouched, and runs blocks $\ell_c{+}1,\dots,\ell_a$ on $N = T_o P + N_f$ tokens;
the action decoder cross-attends to the block-$\ell_a$ output as in the original model~\cite{pai2025mimicvideo}.
The keep ratio $\rho = N_f / (T_f P)$ applies to future tokens only.
We use $\ell_c = 2$, $\ell_a = 20$, $T_o = 2$, $T_f = 14$ and $H{\times}W = 30{\times}40$, so the native sequence has $N_0 = 19{,}200$ tokens.
The backbone and the decoder stay frozen; only the router and a low-rank adapter in the decoder are trained.

\subsection{Action-Aware Router}
\label{sec:router}

The router assigns each future token a relevance score.
A token $x_{t,p}$ of $X_f$ is projected and attends to the instruction tokens $c$, the same text tokens that condition the video DiT;
an MLP maps the result to a gate
\begin{equation}
  g_{t,p} = \sigma\big(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{Attn}(W x_{t,p},\, c,\, c))\big).
  \label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
Allocation is decided once for the whole horizon, so we reduce over time and then over the $2{\times}2$ blocks $\mathcal{B}_j$ of the grid:
\begin{equation}
  s_p = \max_t \, g_{t,p}, \qquad b_j = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_j|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{B}_j} s_p .
  \label{eq:block_score}
\end{equation}
Nothing in \cref{eq:gate} measures visual similarity.
What makes the router action-aware is its training signal, the sensitivity of the decoder's output to each block, not its architecture.
\note{Claude}{Do not call the router instruction-dependent or dynamic: on our diagnostics swapping the instruction leaves the allocation almost unchanged (Spearman 0.96), i.e.\ it behaves as a learned, near-static prior per suite.}
\note{Claude}{The router architecture and its pseudo-label training recipe come from an existing KnowledgeRouter implementation; cite its source if it is published work.}

\subsection{Spatial Resolution Allocation}
\label{sec:spatial}

We partition the grid into $HW/4$ non-overlapping $2{\times}2$ blocks (300 here) and rank them by $b_j$.
The top-$F$ blocks ($F = 100$) keep all four tokens;
each remaining block is replaced by the mean of its four tokens, separately in every frame.
This yields $M = 4F + (HW/4 - F) = 600$ spatial tubes $u_i \in \R^{T_f \times D}$.
Every region of the scene stays represented, at a resolution set by its relevance:
the operator merges rather than deletes, which \cref{sec:analysis} identified as the condition for graceful degradation.
Each tube inherits the score of its block, $a_i = b_{j(i)}$.

\paragraph{Spatial positions.}
Tubes keep their coordinates on the original grid:
a full-resolution tube keeps its own $(h, w)$, and a merged tube takes the coordinate of its block's top-left cell.
We do not re-index merged tubes onto a smaller grid, because spatial positions tell the backbone where content lies in the scene (\cref{sec:analysis}).

\subsection{Temporal Resolution Allocation}
\label{sec:temporal}

Each tube is then compressed along time into $K_i$ segments.
The resolutions come from a few tiers $k_1 < \dots < k_L$ with fixed quotas $n_1, \dots, n_L$, where $\sum_l n_l = M$,
so every sample has exactly $N_f = \sum_l n_l k_l$ future tokens and the budget is met exactly.
Tubes are sorted by the utility
\begin{equation}
  v_i = a_i \, \bar\delta_i ,
  \label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
where $\bar\delta_i$ is the mean cosine distance between consecutive frames of the tube, measured on the router's projected features ($W x$ in \cref{eq:gate}).
The $n_1$ lowest-utility tubes receive $k_1$ segments, the next $n_2$ receive $k_2$, and so on.
A tube is split into $K_i$ contiguous, non-empty segments whose boundaries lie at equal quantiles of its cumulative $\delta$ (solved exactly by dynamic programming),
and each segment is mean-pooled into one token.
For example, $\rho = 10\%$ uses $k = (1, 2, 4)$ and $n = (120, 180, 300)$, giving $N_f = 1{,}680$ and $N = 4{,}080$;
the tiers for all budgets are listed in \cref{sec:exp}.

\paragraph{Temporal positions.}
Unlike the spatial axis, the temporal axis is re-indexed contiguously:
the $k$-th segment of every tube receives temporal index $T_o + k - 1$, whichever source frames it covers.
Keeping source-frame indices instead leaves gaps in the temporal positions and performs markedly worse (\cref{sec:exp}).
This is consistent with \cref{sec:analysis}: future frames carry no frame-specific content at the deployment point, so their temporal positions act as slots the backbone expects to be consecutive.
The two axes thus follow opposite position conventions.
