\paragraph{Routing layer depth}
Originally \method routes after the second of the 20 blocks that a query runs, so only two blocks process all 19,200 tokens. \Cref{tab:supp_depth} tests whether block 2 is a good place to route. It moves the routing point once in each direction and compares each arm with the block-2 cell it would compete with in deployment. Block 1 is the only earlier point where the router still reads the output of a transformer block; any earlier point would have to score the raw patch embeddings. Routing after block 1 runs one fewer block at full resolution , so it is compared with block 2 at the same 4,080 tokens. Block 6 is the deeper point whose estimated cost at 10\% (about 445 ms) matches block 2 at 20\% (440.7 ms). It asks whether the extra compute is better spent on four more full-resolution blocks or on keeping 1,680 more tokens. Points much deeper than block 6 fall outside the range that matters: beyond about block 9, routing at 10\% would cost more than block 2 at 30\% (477.1 ms), and that cell already exceeds the full-context policy on this suite (92.0 vs. 90.4\%).

\paragraph{Router and distillation objectives.}
For the router objective (\cref{tab:supp_objectives}, top), the router is retrained from scratch during Stage~1 with one term changed in the joint training objective: binary cross-entropy instead of the focal loss ($\gamma=0$), $\lambda_b=0$, $\lambda_e=0$, or no pseudo-label term, in which case the router learns only from the task gradient through the straight-through gate (\cref{eq:supp_ste}) plus the budget and entropy terms.
The reference is the full recipe retrained in the same campaign, since retraining itself changes the router; each router is then frozen and given a fresh 10\% adapter in Stage~2.

\paragraph{Spatial and temporal budget allocation.}

\Cref{tab:supp_split} spends the same future-token budget in different ways.
Temporal-only allocation keeps every group at full resolution ($F=300$, 1,200 tubes) and pools only in time; at 10\% this forces 720 tubes to a single segment and the rest to two.
Spatial-only allocation keeps all 14 frames ($K=14$) and merges in space only; merging every group still leaves 4,200 future tokens, so it can only reach the 30\% budget, with $F=20$ (360 tubes).
The two $F$ variants change the number of full-resolution groups at 20\% and rescale the tiers to keep the budget exact: $F=50$ (450 tubes) and $F=150$ (750 tubes).
Their segment quotas are $(90,165,195)$ for $K=(5,7,9)$ at $F=50$, and $(150,270,330)$ for $K=(2,4,6)$ at $F=150$. Both sums equal 3,360 future tokens.

\paragraph{Temporal priority and segment boundaries.}

\Cref{tab:supp_temporal} changes how segment counts are assigned, with the spatial selection unchanged.
Feature change only ranks tubes by $\Delta_p$; reversed priority ranks them by $-u_p$; flat counts give nearly equal segment counts by a fixed spatial pattern; random tier assignment uses a fixed random ranking.




\paragraph{Task-agnostic spatial criteria.}
The five task-agnostic criteria in \cref{fig:supp_relevance}(a) score the same 300 groups of $2\times2$ future-token positions.
Let $z^{(2)}_{t,p}$ denote the full-context video feature after block~2 at future frame $t$ and spatial position $p$, before spatial coarsening.
Each criterion produces one score per group; higher-scoring groups retain full resolution.

\emph{Similarity} uses the negative mean pairwise cosine similarity among the four features within a group, averaged over the 14 future frames.
It therefore retains groups whose features are less redundant.
\emph{Motion} averages $1-\cos(z^{(2)}_{t,p},z^{(2)}_{t+1,p})$ over adjacent future frames and the four positions of the group.
\emph{Block-2 self-attention} averages the attention received from all visual queries over attention heads, then averages the scores of the group's four positions over the future frames.
\emph{Edge energy} applies horizontal and vertical Sobel filters to the channel-mean grayscale image of the last RGB observation and averages the gradient magnitude over each group's image region.
\emph{Random} assigns an independent uniform score in $[0,1]$ to each group using a reproducibly seeded draw for each held-out window.

\paragraph{Offline coarsening protocol.}
For each of the 200 held-out windows from 50 episodes, each criterion's ranking is computed once and its top-$n$ groups retain full resolution for $n\in\{25,50,100,150,200,250\}$.
The remaining groups are replaced by their spatial means at their top-left source positions.
All 14 future frames are retained, so these curves vary spatial coarsening alone.
Every criterion uses the same observation, video-noise draw, action-noise seed, and original action decoder.
The plotted distortion is the geometric mean, across windows, of the mean squared error between compressed-context and full-context normalized actions over the first five executed actions.

\paragraph{Insertion curves and ranking reversal.}
\Cref{fig:supp_relevance} examines how the learned ranking affects action preservation and closed-loop control.
With 50--250 groups kept at full resolution, router-guided insertion gives the lowest action distortion among the compared criteria.
Reversing the scores to $1-S_g$ increases distortion and reduces success at all three token budgets, even with a separately retrained decoder adapter.
